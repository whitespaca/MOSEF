\title{Order-Separating Exponent Families for Classical Integer
Factorization: Algorithms, Barriers, and Reproducible Evaluation}
\author{MOSEF Research Program}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
This manuscript defines an evidence-driven program for evaluating
order-separating exponent families in classical integer factorization. It
proves the elementary support-separator implication and an exact
valuation-aware criterion for repeated prime factors. The original
support-only requirement cannot cover prime powers and is refuted as an
all-composite target. On an explicit hereditary semismooth-order promise
class, we prove a Las Vegas complete-factorization theorem with polynomial
expected bit complexity. The promise is factor dependent and is not
recognized by the algorithm. We also prove that divisor coverage, even by a
positive difference family, does not imply multiplicative-order separation:
the missing condition is injectivity of divisibility signatures. No general
polynomial-time factoring theorem is claimed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Let \(N\) be a positive integer represented in binary. The project asks whether
order information from one or more algebraic groups can expose a nontrivial
factor without circular access to the factorization. General classical
polynomial-time factorization is a research target, not a premise.

The immediate objective is methodological: every mathematical statement is
linked to a status and evidence record, finite experiments are not promoted to
universal theorems, and complexity is measured in binary input length.

\section{Model and definitions}

\claimstatus{DEF-001}{DEFINITION}. The input length is
\[
  m=\left\lceil\log_2 N\right\rceil.
\]
Thus a running time polynomial in \(N\) is not, merely for that reason,
polynomial in the input length.

\claimstatus{DEF-002}{DEFINITION}. Let \(P(N)\) be the set of distinct prime
divisors of \(N\). For \(\gcd(g,N)=1\) and \(d>0\), define
\[
  D_N(g,d)=\{p\in P(N):\operatorname{ord}_p(g)\mid d\}.
\]
The pair \((g,d)\) is an order separator when \(D_N(g,d)\) is nonempty and is
not all of \(P(N)\).

\claimstatus{DEF-003}{DEFINITION}. If
\(N=\prod_{p\in P(N)}p^{e_p}\), define
\[
  a_p(N,g,d)=\min\{e_p,\nu_p(g^d-1)\},
\]
where \(\nu_p(0)=+\infty\). A valuation separator has at least one
\(a_p>0\) and at least one \(a_q<e_q\), where \(p=q\) is allowed.

A polynomial order-separating family must bound not only the number of bases
and exponents, but also construction cost, exponent bit lengths, evaluation
cost, and recursive factor extraction in \(\operatorname{poly}(m)\) bit
operations.

\section{Inspected baseline evidence}

\claimstatus{EXT-001}{PROVED, IMPORTED}. Agrawal, Kayal, and Saxena give an
unconditional deterministic polynomial-time primality test
\cite{agrawal2004primes}. This decision result must not be restated as a
factor-finding algorithm.

\claimstatus{EXT-002}{PROVED, IMPORTED}. Lenstra and Pomerance prove a
probabilistic complete-factorization algorithm with expected running time
\(L_N[1/2,1+o(1)]\) \cite{lenstra1992rigorous}. This is a rigorous
subexponential bound, not a polynomial bound in \(m\).

Lenstra's elliptic-curve method motivates varying the group channel, while the
expected-time expression highlighted in the inspected abstract is explicitly
conjectural \cite{lenstra1987elliptic}. It therefore supplies motivation, not a
separation theorem for MOSEF.

\section{Open research target}

\claimstatus{OPEN-001}{REFUTED}. A support-defined family cannot guarantee an
order separator for every composite input. If \(N=p^e\), then
\(P(N)=\{p\}\), so \(D_N(g,d)\) is either empty or all of \(P(N)\), never
nonempty and proper.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\claimstatus{OPEN-002}{OPEN}.

It remains unknown here whether exact polynomial time primality and
perfect-power preprocessing can be followed by a support-POSF constructor,
independent of the factorization, that succeeds on every residual composite
cofactor within all polynomial representation, construction, evaluation, and
recursion bounds.
\end{minipage}

\claimstatus{OPEN-003}{OPEN}. It also remains unknown whether an all-input
valuation-separating family satisfying the same bounds can be constructed
without access to the factorization.

\section{Separator criteria and algorithmic framework}

\claimstatus{LEM-001}{PROVED}. If \(D_N(g,d)\) is nonempty and proper, choose
\(p\in D_N(g,d)\) and \(q\in P(N)\setminus D_N(g,d)\). Then \(p\) divides
\(\gcd(g^d-1,N)\), while \(q\) does not. Hence
\[
  1<\gcd(g^d-1,N)<N.
\]
The argument is unchanged when \(N\) has repeated prime factors.

\claimstatus{LEM-002}{PROVED}. Prime valuations give the exact identity
\[
  \gcd(g^d-1,N)=
  \prod_{p\in P(N)}p^{\min\{e_p,\nu_p(g^d-1)\}}.
\]
Thus the GCD is nontrivial exactly when the capped profile is neither all zero
nor the full profile \((e_p)\). For square-free \(N\), this is equivalent to
the support condition. For \(N=p^e\), it instead reduces to
\(0<\nu_p(g^d-1)<e\).

One candidate first computes \(\gcd(g,N)\), returning any direct factor and
rejecting a base divisible by \(N\). For a unit base it computes
\(r=g^d\bmod N\) and classifies \(\gcd(r-1,N)\) as a miss, a nontrivial
factor, or a simultaneous collision equal to \(N\). A family failure is
reported as unresolved, never prime.

The complete wrapper validates primality, recursively expands exact perfect
powers, invokes a constructor only on residual composites, validates every
reported split, and propagates construction or unresolved failures. The full
pseudocode and proof are in:
\begin{center}
\small\path{research/proofs/M2-formal-specification.md}.
\end{center}

Let \(C(m)\) bound explicit construction, let \(B(m)\) and \(E(m)\) bound the
numbers of canonical residue bases and exponents, and let \(L(m)\) bound
exponent bit length. With multiplication cost \(M(m)\) and GCD cost \(G(m)\),
one Cartesian-product pass costs
\[
  O\!\left(C(m)+B(m)E(m)
  \left(M(m)L(m)+G(m)\right)\right).
\]
The factor tree has fewer than \(2m\) nodes. This gives a polynomial framework
only conditionally on polynomial \(C,B,E,L\), polynomial preprocessing, and a
success guarantee on every recursive cofactor; no such universal constructor
is supplied.

\section{Prime-power obstruction and finite falsification}

The smallest valuation-only witness is
\[
  (N,g,d)=(4,3,1),\qquad
  D_4(3,1)=P(4),\qquad
  \gcd(3-1,4)=2.
\]
The smallest odd witness is \((9,2,2)\). These examples do not refute
LEM-001; they refute the converse outside the square-free domain and expose
the impossible scope of the original all-composite support-POSF target.

\claimstatus{EMP-002}{EMPIRICAL}. A deterministic search over composite
\(4\le N\le500\), unit bases \(2\le g\le20\), and \(1\le d\le20\)
evaluated 78,860 candidates. The registered box produced no counterexample to
LEM-001, LEM-002, or square-free equivalence, and found 5,672
nonsquarefree valuation-only splits. Twenty-four selected Python, Rust, and
C\# comparisons also passed. This finite agreement is an audit, not a proof.

\section{A restricted semismooth-order theorem}

Let \(B(k)\) and \(R(k)\) be nondecreasing, polynomially bounded integer
functions computable in polynomial time, and define
\[
  M_B(k)=\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,B(k)).
\]
\claimstatus{DEF-004}{DEFINITION}. A composite non-perfect-power \(K\), of
bit length \(k\), has a semismooth asymmetry witness when it has distinct
prime divisors \(p,q\) and an integer \(1\le t\le R(k)\) such that
\[
  p-1\mid tM_B(k)
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  q-1\nmid tM_B(k).
\]
An input is in the hereditary promise class when every composite
non-perfect-power divisor has such a witness at its local bit length.
Membership is promised, not recognized: the definition may use the
factorization, but the algorithm does not.

\claimstatus{THM-001}{PROVED}. On this hereditary class, a classical Las
Vegas algorithm returns the complete prime factorization with probability one,
is always correct, and has expected running time polynomial in the binary
input length.

At a residual composite \(K\), the algorithm repeatedly enumerates
\(1\le t\le R(k)\). For each \(t\), it samples a uniform residue
\(a\bmod K\), first tries \(\gcd(a,K)\), and then tries
\[
  \gcd\!\left(a^{tM_B(k)}-1,K\right).
\]
Fix a witness \((p,q,t)\) and write \(d=tM_B(k)\). Every unit satisfies
\(a^d=1\bmod p\). The unit group modulo \(q\) is cyclic, and \(x^d=1\)
has \(\gcd(d,q-1)\le(q-1)/2\) solutions because \(q-1\nmid d\).
Thus at least half the units separate \(p\) from \(q\). All nonzero,
nonunit residues already expose a proper GCD. Consequently
\[
  \Pr[\text{one witness trial succeeds}]
  \ge \frac{K-1-\varphi(K)/2}{K}
  \ge \frac{K-1}{2K}
  \ge \frac{5}{12},
\]
where the last inequality uses \(K\ge6\). A full pass therefore succeeds with
conditional probability at least \(5/12\), so it needs at most \(12/5\)
passes in expectation.

The exponent has
\[
  O(B(k)\log B(k)+\log R(k))
\]
bits because \(M_B(k)\mid B(k)!\). Exact rejection sampling takes fewer than
two \(k\)-bit draws in expectation. Deterministic polynomial-time primality,
exact maximal-exponent perfect-power preprocessing, and fewer than \(4m\)
recursive invocations complete the expected polynomial bit-complexity proof.
The full proof, including recursive correctness and outside-promise semantics,
is in \path{research/proofs/THM-001-semismooth-promise.md}.

\claimstatus{REF-001}{REFUTED}. The condition \(q-1\nmid d\) does not make a
fixed base deterministic. For
\[
  N=51,\quad p=3,\quad q=17,\quad
  d=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,8)=840,
\]
we have \(p-1\mid d\), \(q-1\nmid d\), but
\(\operatorname{ord}_{17}(2)=8\mid d\). Hence
\(\gcd(2^{840}-1,51)=51\), a simultaneous collision. Random residues repair
this proper-subgroup failure probabilistically; the false fixed-base
statement remains refuted.

\claimstatus{EMP-003}{EMPIRICAL}. With \(B=8\), \(R=3\), and
\(4\le N\le500\), exact enumeration found 155 hereditary promised inputs and
factored all of them with the finite residue oracle. It checked all 557
ordered witness tuples in the box; the smallest observed exact success
probability was \(268/493\), above the proved \(5/12\) bound. Twenty-two
selected Python, Rust, and C\# comparisons passed. These finite checks
falsify implementations, not prove the theorem.

\section{Difference coverage versus order separation}

For a finite positive candidate family \(\Delta\), define
\[
  \Sigma_\Delta(r)=\{d\in\Delta:r\mid d\}.
\]
\claimstatus{DEF-005}{DEFINITION}. The family has the \(n\)-divisor property
when every signature for \(1\le r\le n\) is nonempty. It separates an order
profile \((r_1,\ldots,r_k)\) when some \(d\in\Delta\) is divisible by at least
one but not all of the profile orders.

Umans and Wang conjecture structured difference sets \(S-T\) having the
\(n\)-divisor property, with magnitude, cardinality, and
generalized-arithmetic-progression bounds \cite{umans2025numbertheoretic}.
Their integer algorithm uses divisibility of actual small prime factors into
prefactored differences and does not use multiplicative orders.
\claimstatus{EXT-003}{CONDITIONAL, IMPORTED}. Their Theorem~5.5 states,
assuming the Strong Prefactored Divisor Conjecture, a deterministic
\[
  N^{\max(\alpha,\beta)/2+o(1)}
\]
integer-factorization running time. This remains exponential in
\(m=\lceil\log_2N\rceil\). The inspected version also leaves uniformity,
modulus-range, and sign/zero conventions insufficiently explicit; the source
note records these textual gaps. We therefore neither treat the construction
as available nor restate the bound as unconditional.

\claimstatus{BAR-001}{PROVED}. Divisor coverage does not imply order
separation, including for positive difference families. A candidate \(d\)
separates a profile if and only if its signatures are not all equal: a hit
and a miss distinguish two signatures, and any element of the symmetric
difference of two signatures supplies a hit and a miss. Consequently a family
separates every profile containing distinct orders from \(\{1,\ldots,n\}\)
if and only if \(r\mapsto\Sigma_\Delta(r)\) is injective on that interval.
Coverage requires only nonempty signatures.

The minimal difference-family counterexample is
\[
  S=\{3\},\qquad T=\{1\},\qquad
  \Delta^+(S,T)=\{2\}.
\]
Both 1 and 2 divide the sole difference, but
\(\Sigma_\Delta(1)=\Sigma_\Delta(2)\). This occurs in the multiplicative-order
mechanism itself:
\[
  \operatorname{ord}_2(5)=1,\quad
  \operatorname{ord}_3(5)=2,\quad
  \gcd(5^2-1,6)=6.
\]
Thus the covered candidate is a simultaneous collision, not a factor. The
smallest odd example is \(N=15,g=4,d=2\).

If a family has both coverage and injective signatures on
\(\{1,\ldots,n\}\), its \(n\) signatures are distinct nonempty subsets of
\(\Delta\). Hence
\[
  n\le2^{|\Delta|}-1,\qquad
  |\Delta|\ge\lceil\log_2(n+1)\rceil.
\]
For \(\Delta=\Delta^+(S,T)\), also
\(|S||T|\ge|\Delta|\). This is only an explicit-candidate counting bound; it
does not exclude compact batch evaluation.

A control construction shows sufficiency without efficiency. Put
\(h=\lceil\sqrt n\rceil\),
\[
  S=\{1+ih:1\le i\le h\},\qquad
  T=\{1+j:0\le j<h\}.
\]
Its positive differences are exactly \(\{1,\ldots,h^2\}\), so for distinct
\(r<s\le n\), candidate \(d=r\) separates the two. The construction uses
\(h^2=\Theta(n)\) pairs, exponential when \(n\) has magnitude exponential in
the input bit length. It is a correctness control, not a polynomial-time
POSF.

\claimstatus{REF-002}{REFUTED}. The attempted implication
``\(n\)-divisor difference cover \(\Rightarrow\) distinct-order separator''
is false. This does not refute the conditional Umans--Wang algorithms, whose
collision-resolution mechanism is different.

\claimstatus{EMP-004}{EMPIRICAL}. Exhaustive enumeration checked all 4,095
nonempty candidate subsets of \(\{1,\ldots,12\}\) against orders through 8,
including 114,660 two-order profiles. Among 576 divisor covers, 240 had
noninjective signatures; 336 were injective. The signature characterization,
counting bound, and square construction through \(n=200\) passed, as did
twelve selected Python, Rust, and C\# comparisons. The canonical summary hash
and exact commands are recorded in
\path{research/experiments/EXP-0004-m4-difference-cover-search.md}.

\section{Baseline algorithm suite}

The M1 suite contains exact modular exponentiation, trial division,
perfect-power detection, exact trial-division primality for validation,
bounded Pollard rho, Pollard \(p-1\) stage 1, a scoped Williams-style
\(p+1\) stage 1 using the Lucas sequence \(V_k(P,1)\), and per-item batch
GCD semantics.

Python supplies the arbitrary-precision reference behavior. Rust supplies the
authoritative implementation only on its documented \texttt{u64} domain, with
\texttt{u128} products preventing modular-multiplication overflow. An
independent C\# \texttt{BigInteger} program verifies selected operations. The
current batch-GCD function is a semantic baseline and is not a product-tree
performance claim.

\claimstatus{EMP-001}{EMPIRICAL}. On the finite canonical corpus in
\path{schemas/baseline-vectors-v1.json}, 58 cross-language comparisons
passed. Rust agreed with Python for every registered operation; C\# also agreed
for modular exponentiation, validation primality, trial factors, and per-item
batch GCDs. This finite agreement neither proves asymptotic complexity nor
establishes correctness outside the specified domains.

\section{Limitations}

No universal support-POSF or valuation-family constructor is claimed. THM-001
applies only on an explicitly factor-dependent hereditary promise and gives no
membership recognizer or density result. Outside that promise its unbounded
randomized splitter may not terminate. BAR-001 is not a lower bound for all
compressed or algebraic candidate evaluators, and the factor-dependent order
profile is not an algorithmic input. The imported difference-cover conjecture
is neither proved nor a polynomial-bit-complexity result. Trial-division
validation primality is intentionally not polynomial in binary input length,
the Rust algorithms are bounded to \texttt{u64}, and finite searches are not
proofs for untested inputs. The literature set is not a
current-state-of-the-art survey.

\section{Conclusion}

The M0 foundation makes claim status, source inspection, and experiment
metadata explicit, and M1 provides a differentially checked small-input
baseline. M2 proves the support and valuation criteria, specifies all
candidate and recursive failure branches, and replaces an impossible
all-composite support target with two explicit open formulations. M3 supplies
a noncircular Las Vegas theorem for a hereditary semismooth-order promise
class while preserving the minimal fixed-base collision that blocks a
deterministic shortcut. M4 proves that divisor coverage and order separation
are different combinatorial requirements: injective divisibility signatures
are necessary and sufficient for distinct-order profiles, and the smallest
covered difference family already fails them. This barrier narrows the next
search without resolving general classical factoring.
